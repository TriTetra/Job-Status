# Score jobs by chance-weighted surplus

A candidate-side job scorer should stop ranking postings by a single "match" number. It should rank each posting by **expected surplus**: the probability that the posting is real and the candidate gets an interview, multiplied by how far the job's value exceeds the candidate's reservation value. The reservation value should rise as the chance falls, because every application costs effort. This is the welfare-optimal index derived and tested in two randomized field experiments with the French public employment service. There, recommendations that combined "a job I like" with "a job I can get" beat either signal alone, and the vacancy with the highest utility ranked only about 4,403rd by hiring probability ([Bied et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21699)). The research also says "fit" should be split, not averaged. Demands–abilities signals (title, must-have skills, seniority) belong in the chance model, because recruiters spend about 7.4 seconds on a first pass and perceived person–job fit correlates .67 with intent to hire. Needs–supplies signals (pay, remote work, growth) belong in the value model, because they predict satisfaction about 1.5 times as strongly. For a non-EU software/AI candidate, the biggest swings in chance come from legal gates and congestion, not skill overlap. UK sponsorship needs a graduate-level role at £41,700 or more and costs the employer £1,320 a year in levy. Swiss third-country permits are capped at 8,500 a year. Worldwide-remote postings draw 8.8 times the average number of applicants. About 18–22% of postings on a major ATS have no intent to hire. The recommended v1 pipeline runs in order: conjunctive gates, then a multiplicative chance model on a roughly 8% cold-interview base rate, then a log-pay-led value score with floors, then the expected-surplus ranking, then a greedy shortlist. Most individual multipliers below are **design choices anchored on evidence, not measured effects**. No public dataset links requirement coverage, posting age or sponsorship wording to callback rates, so the system must log outcomes from day one and replace the priors with calibrated estimates.

## Recruiters reward perceived job fit; candidates need supplies fit

The largest meta-analysis of fit covers 172 studies and 836 effect sizes. It shows that fit predicts the hiring decision far better than it predicts anything that happens after hiring ([Kristof-Brown, Zimmerman & Johnson 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)). **Person–job (PJ) fit correlates ρ≈.67 with an organization's intent to hire, and person–organization (PO) fit ρ≈.61.** Their links to later performance are weak: .20 for PJ, with an 80% credibility interval from −.04 to .44, and .07 for PO. Their links to actual turnover are close to zero (−.08 and −.14). How fit is measured matters even more than which type is measured. PO fit predicts job offers at **.50 when the recruiter perceives it directly and .03 when it is computed indirectly**. The authors conclude that these perceptions have little connection with objective reality and may reflect "similar-to-me" bias. An algorithm's match score is an indirect, objective measure. So it should be read as a proxy for what a screener will *perceive* in a few seconds, not as a measure of how well the candidate would really fit.

The same paper also says what drives the candidate's own outcomes. Needs–supplies fit (does the job supply what the person wants?) correlates **.61 with job satisfaction, against .41 for demands–abilities fit**. For intent to quit the figures are **−.50 against −.23**, so needs–supplies fit is about 1.5 times as predictive for satisfaction and about 2 times for quitting. Demands–abilities fit relates to PO fit at only .45, while needs–supplies fit relates to it at .73 ([Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)). The structural redesign follows directly. **Skills, title and seniority overlap should feed only the chance model. Pay, flexibility, growth and domain interest should feed only the value model.** A separate "fit" multiplier on top of both would count the same evidence twice. One caution: the meta-analysis notes that excess environment is generally less harmful than excess person in polynomial-regression studies. That finding concerns attitudes and is ambiguous for qualifications, so the asymmetric qualification penalty proposed below rests on field experiments instead.

Selection-validity research supports the decision to model what screeners do rather than what predicts success. Pre-hire work experience predicts job performance at only about ρ≈.06 ([Van Iddekinge et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12335)), and years of experience reached only .18 in the classic validity tables ([Schmidt & Hunter 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.124.2.262)); these figures were not re-verified in this research run. Yet employers still filter on years. The score should therefore be described to users as "likely to be screened in", never as "you will succeed here".

## Interview chance starts near 8% and moves by multiplicative gates

The first human screen is extremely short. In Ladders' eye-tracking studies, recruiters spent about **6 seconds per resume in 2012 and 7.4 seconds in 2018**, and they looked at job titles more than any other element ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)). Before that human pass, ATS filters act as knockouts. In the Harvard Business School–Accenture survey of 2,275 executives in the US, UK and Germany, **88% of employers said qualified high-skill candidates are rejected because they don't exactly match the criteria (94% for middle-skill candidates)**, largely because of "negative" filters ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/how-to-tap-the-talent-automated-hr-platforms-miss)). Jobscan's tracking of more than 2.5M applications found an exact title match associated with **10.6 times the interview rate**, and a cover letter with 3.4 times ([Jobscan 2025](https://jobscan.co/blog/interview-rates-study)). Those figures are heavily confounded by self-selection, so they are useful only for ordering: title matters more than a cover letter. Together these findings imply two kinds of input. Explicit hard requirements (work authorization, onsite location, a required language, an enforced degree) are near-binary gates. Title and seniority alignment is a heavy multiplier, not one of many equally weighted features.

Skill coverage below 100% should not be punished as hard as folk wisdom suggests. The famous "men apply at 60%, women only at 100%" statistic comes from an unpublished internal HP report. The UK Behavioural Insights Team calls it frequently cited but unsubstantiated ([BIT 2022](https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Gender-differences-in-response-to-requirements-in-job-adverts-March-2022.pdf)). LinkedIn's behavioral data suggests self-screening is too strict: women apply to about **20% fewer jobs but are 16% more likely to be hired** when they do apply ([LinkedIn 2019](https://business.linkedin.com/talent-solutions/blog/diversity/2019/how-women-find-jobs-gender-report)). On the employer side, **73% of HR professionals say fewer than half of applicants meet all listed criteria** ([LinkedIn, Jan 2025](https://news.linkedin.com/2025/talent-campaign-b2b-press-release)), so a candidate who meets most of them is already above the median applicant. No public dataset reports interview rates by share of requirements met. The functional form is therefore a smooth, saturating logistic curve on weighted must-have coverage, with no cliff at 60% and no requirement of 100%. Nice-to-haves get only a small additive effect.

Over- and under-qualification are not symmetric. In a Belgian correspondence experiment, **master's graduates applying to bachelor-level jobs got 19% more direct interview invitations** and 11% more positive reactions, with larger gains in shortage occupations ([Verhaest et al. 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:indres:v:57:y:2018:i:3:p:361-388)). A companion study across 540 vacancies found interview rates of **12% for school-leavers, 10% for formerly overeducated applicants and 8% for formerly unemployed applicants** ([Baert & Verhaest 2014](https://repec.iza.org/dp8312.pdf)). Lab experiments with US hiring managers show the limit. Clearly overqualified candidates are judged less committed and a flight risk, and are penalized for it, although the effect reverses for women in child-rearing years ([Hahl et al. 2019, via CMU](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2019/july/overqualified-job-seekers.html)). The resulting shape is a **neutral-to-slightly-positive band one step above the requirement, a moderate penalty two or more steps above, and a steep penalty below**. No field study isolates callbacks by years of experience short of a stated "X+ years", so the size of the under-qualification penalty is a prior to be learned.

Referrals are the largest multiplier a candidate can control. Lever's data puts hire odds at roughly **1 in 16 for referrals against 1 in 152 for career-site applicants** ([SHRM on Lever](https://www.shrm.org/resourcesandtools/hr-topics/talent-acquisition/pages/lever-study-candidates-hired.aspx)). Ashby reports **40% of referred candidates reach interview** ([Ashby](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals)). In ERIN's 2024 referral funnel, 4 of 6 referred applicants were interviewed ([SHRM on ERIN](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/employee-referrals-remain-top-source-hires)).

Cold-application baselines are low:

- **Bertrand & Mullainathan:** a 9.65% callback rate ([NBER w9873](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9873); not re-verified this run).
- **Belgian experiments:** 8–12% interview invitations ([Baert & Verhaest 2014](https://repec.iza.org/dp8312.pdf)).
- **French employment service:** median success probabilities of 0.02–0.06 per application ([Bied et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21699)).

A **cold-interview base rate of b = 0.08** for a fully qualified applicant fits these figures. It also passes a consistency check. If about 8% of interviews convert to offers, then 0.08 × 0.08 ≈ 1/156, close to Lever's 1/152 hire rate. And a ×5 referral multiplier reproduces Ashby's 40%. The interview chance is then a product:

```
P_interview = min(0.60, b · G · f_match · m_title · m_qual · m_ref · m_time · m_comp · m_market)
f_match     = logistic(10 · (c_must − 0.55)) · (0.9 + 0.1 · c_nice)
P_eff       = P_real(age, signals) · P_interview
```

Here G ∈ {0,1} is the conjunctive gate, c_must is the importance-weighted share of must-have skills covered, and c_nice is the share of nice-to-haves covered. The 0.60 ceiling sits just below ERIN's referred-interview rate of about 67%. Because the base rate is small, multiplying probabilities is a close approximation to multiplying odds. An implementation can work in log-odds to stay exact.

| Chance factor | Recommended value | Evidence anchor | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base cold interview rate b | 0.08 | Callback rates of 8–12% ([IZA](https://repec.iza.org/dp8312.pdf)); consistent with 1/152 hires ([SHRM/Lever](https://www.shrm.org/resourcesandtools/hr-topics/talent-acquisition/pages/lever-study-candidates-hired.aspx)) | Calibrated prior |
| Must-have coverage f_match | Logistic, midpoint 0.55, slope 10: 0.95 at 85% coverage, 0.18 at 40% | 60/100 rule unsubstantiated ([BIT](https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Gender-differences-in-response-to-requirements-in-job-adverts-March-2022.pdf)) | Design choice |
| Title family and level m_title | Same family and level 1.0; adjacent 0.6; unrelated 0.25 | Titles dominate the screen ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)) | Design choice |
| Under-qualification m_qual | ×0.75 per year short of stated minimum; enforced degree missing = gate | No direct field study | Design choice |
| Over-qualification m_qual | One step over 1.0 (1.1 in shortage fields); two or more steps over 0.7 | +19% invitations ([Verhaest](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:indres:v:57:y:2018:i:3:p:361-388)); flight-risk penalty ([Hahl](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2019/july/overqualified-job-seekers.html)) | Evidence-shaped |
| Referral m_ref | ×5, capped by the 0.60 ceiling | 40% referred to interview ([Ashby](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals)) | Evidence-shaped |

## Timing, crowding and ghost postings shrink the odds further

The only quantified decay curve for posting age is weak but directionally consistent. TalentWorks found that applying within the **first 96 hours gave up to 8 times better interview odds, with chances falling about 28% per day afterwards** ([summary of TalentWorks](https://paulvanderlaken.com/2017/10/24/talent-works-data-science-to-improve-job-application-chances/)). It used a small sample from around 2017, with no primary report. Ashby's data on about 13M applications explains the mechanism: **week one draws 2–3 times the applications of later weeks** ([Ashby](https://www.ashbyhq.com/blog/recruiting/2023-trends-report-applications-per-job)), so the early pile gets screened before later applicants arrive. Recruiters keep screening long after that, though. Median time from opening to first hire is **56 days for business roles and 76 for technical roles** ([Ashby benchmarks](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/recruiting-operations-benchmarks-talent-trends?view=full)). Compounding −28% per day indefinitely would leave just 10% of the baseline after one extra week, which is too aggressive for roles that stay open for two months.

The recommended form is two-phase. The multiplier stays at 1.0 through day 4. After that it is **m_time = max(0.3, 0.88^(d−4)) for technical roles**, reaching the floor around day 13, and max(0.3, 0.85^(d−4)) for business roles, which fill faster. TalentWorks' 0.72 daily rate is the aggressive upper bound. The posting-age factor should also drive an "apply now" urgency flag, not just a lower score.

Congestion is the second discount. Applications per job roughly **tripled from 2021 to 2024**, to more than 300 per role ([HR Dive on Ashby](https://www.hrdive.com/news/recruiters-see-job-applications-triple-to-more-than-300-per-role/820096/)). Remote roles absorb a disproportionate share. In the US at the end of 2023, **remote jobs were 10% of LinkedIn postings but drew 46% of applications** ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/01/29/remote-jobs-scarce-many-applications-labor-market-linkedin-report/)). That implies roughly 7–8 times the applicants per remote posting compared with a non-remote one. Jobgether's September 2026 index of 200,043 remote postings is the most relevant source for this candidate. **Worldwide postings drew 8.8 times the average applications, region-wide postings ("Europe", "EMEA") 3.7 times, and single-country postings 0.66 times** ([Jobgether](https://jobgether.com/blog/remote-hiring-trends-september-2026)). Chance should not scale one-for-one with applicant count, because much of the extra volume is low-fit. **38% of job seekers admit to mass-applying** ([Greenhouse 2024](https://www.greenhouse.com/blog/greenhouse-2024-state-of-job-hunting-report)), and most applicants miss some listed criteria. A square-root elasticity, **m_comp = (A / A_ref)^(−0.5)**, is a defensible prior. It gives region-wide remote roles 0.42 and worldwide roles 0.27, relative to single-country or onsite roles. Where a board shows an applicant count, use it directly: (N/250)^(−0.5), clipped to [0.3, 1.5]. Famous brands deserve a further discount of about 0.6. The often-quoted 0.2% Google acceptance rate comes from a 2014 remark and is weak evidence ([Quartz](https://qz.com/285001/heres-why-you-only-have-a-0-2-chance-of-getting-hired-at-google)).

Ghost postings call for a separate factor, P_real, because a posting with no intent to hire has zero chance however well the candidate fits it. Greenhouse classified **18–22% of jobs posted on its platform each quarter as ghost jobs** in 2024 (18.9% in Q2), and about 70% of employers listed at least one; it did not disclose its method ([Greenhouse](https://www.greenhouse.com/blog/greenhouse-2024-state-of-job-hunting-report)). Revelio Labs found **hires per posting fell from 0.75 in 2018 to under 0.5 in 2023** ([Revelio Labs](https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/macro/ghost-job-postings/)). Indeed's career-trends expert disputes that ghost jobs are prevalent ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2024/06/07/1197965117/ghost-jobs)), so the prior should be moderate, not alarmist.

Age is the most usable signal. Kickresume's snapshot of 96,902 Indeed ads in September 2026 found **34% older than 30 days, and 43% in technology** ([Kickresume](https://www.kickresume.com/en/press/ghost-jobs-study/)). A February 2026 scrape found only **4% active for 4 months or longer** ([Clarify Capital](https://clarifycapital.com/ghost-jobs-2026)). Exact reposts were only 0.1%, but reposting can reset a posting's displayed age. Age should therefore be measured from the first date the tool saw the normalized job (company + title + location), never from the posting date shown on the board.

The stepped table below averages to about 0.78 over that age distribution, which reproduces Greenhouse's ghost prior. The age effects in m_time and P_real compound, but their combined floor should not fall below about 0.1, because they partly measure the same thing.

| First-seen age | P_real | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| 0–14 days | 0.90 | Fresh; inside the week-one application surge |
| 15–30 days | 0.85 | Still well inside the 56–76 day median fill time |
| 31–60 days | 0.70 | Older than about two-thirds of live ads |
| 61–120 days | 0.50 | Beyond Ashby's median technical fill time |
| >120 days | 0.30 | Only about 4% of listings survive this long |

Secondary ghost signals have not been validated by any published study, so each should carry a mild factor:

| Ghost signal | Multiplier on P_real |
|---|---|
| No salary in a jurisdiction where pay disclosure is mandatory | ×0.85 |
| Same normalized job re-posted after more than 60 days | ×0.8 |
| Role missing from the employer's own careers page or ATS | ×0.8 |
| Evergreen or "talent pool" wording | ×0.5 |

## Visa and remote rules turn geography into gates

For a candidate based in Turkey or Central Asia, the target market sets the chance ceiling before skills come into play.

**United Kingdom.** The UK is now the most restrictive of the four country markets.

- **Thresholds:** since 22 July 2025, Skilled Worker sponsorship needs a graduate-level (RQF 6) role and a salary of at least **£41,700** ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2025/07/03/new-uk-immigration-work-route-restrictions-from-22-july-2025)).
- **Employer cost:** since 16 December 2025, large sponsors pay an Immigration Skills Charge of **£1,320 per worker per year**, upfront, which comes to about £6,600 for a five-year visa ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/fr/pubs/2025/10/uk-announces-significant-increase-to-immigration-skills-charge-and-reforms-to-work-routes)).
- **Volume:** overseas tech applications fell for the third straight year, to **34,936 in 2025**, including **15,689 certificates for programmers and software developers** ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/a/5288476)).
- **Sponsor register:** about **126,104 organisations** held a licence in mid-2026 ([DavidsonMorris](https://www.davidsonmorris.com/?p=119290)). A UK posting that says nothing about sponsorship is therefore a long shot unless the employer is on the register. A posting below £41,700 for a role not on the Immigration Salary List is legally unsponsorable for a new entrant.

**Germany.** Germany is the most open market. The 2026 EU Blue Card thresholds are **€50,700, and €45,934.20 for shortage occupations, which include ICT** ([Aldag Legal](https://aldaglegal.com/en/eu-blue-card-germany-2026/)). Sources report that IT specialists can qualify through experience without a degree. German "sponsorship" carries no employer levy. The Opportunity Card lets a candidate job-hunt from inside Germany, but needs **€13,092 in blocked funds** ([Bundesagentur für Arbeit](https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/vor-ort/zav/working-and-living-in-germany/iss-en/issue-02-2026/opportunity-card)). It is a candidate-side lever, not an employer signal.

**Switzerland.** Switzerland caps third-country permits at **8,500 a year (4,500 B + 4,000 L)**, unchanged for 2026. Only about 52% of the 2025 quota had been used by September ([SEM](https://www.sem.admin.ch/en/newnsb/7HwBjdg5HpBA)). The binding constraint is therefore the employer's burden of proving it could not find a Swiss or EU candidate, not the cap itself. That burden makes small Swiss startups close to impossible.

**Spain.** Spain set its Blue Card minimum at **€39,269.92** in January 2026 ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/our-insights/gms-flash-alert/2026/flash-alert-2026-054.html)). Secondary sources report a rise in May 2026 to €41,356.36, with a shortage rate of €33,085.09 ([Jobbatical](https://www.jobbatical.com/blog/spain-eu-blue-card-salary-drop-mid-contract)). The Digital Nomad Visa needs **€2,849 a month, with at least 80% of income from non-Spanish sources** ([The Local Spain](https://www.thelocal.es/20260216/confirmed-the-financial-requirements-for-spains-digital-nomad-visa-in-2026)). It opens a door after a candidate lands a foreign remote contract, not at application time.

**"Remote in Europe".** This label usually means "wherever we already have an entity or an Employer of Record (EOR)". Truly worldwide postings are only **about 2.1%** of remote postings once mis-tagged "anywhere" postings are removed; region-wide postings are 4.4% ([Jobgether](https://jobgether.com/blog/remote-hiring-trends-september-2026)). Employers restrict location because of **permanent-establishment** risk, a taxable business presence created by a remote worker abroad, along with payroll-registration duties ([Thomson Reuters](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/blog/payroll-pulse-navigating-permanent-establishment-risk-with-remote-workers-in-2026/)). An EOR fixes this for about **$599–699 per employee per month** ([Deel pricing](https://www.deel.com/en-gb/pricing/)), a 10–20% cost premium on a mid-level salary.

**Contracting from Turkey.** Turkey gives this candidate a cheaper route. Presidential Decree No. 11257 reportedly raised the deduction on qualifying software and engineering service exports **from 80% to 100%**, provided proceeds are brought into a Turkish bank ([EY](https://www.ey.com/en_gl/technical/tax-alerts/turkiye-amends-certain-income-tax-and-corporate-tax-exemptions-and-deductions)). Postings that welcome contractors or B2B arrangements are therefore realistic. The same tax rule should raise *value* too, because the score should use net income, not gross.

No study measures how often "visa sponsorship available" postings actually hire from abroad. The market multipliers below are inferences from legal mechanics, intended as priors.

| Market and posting signal | m_market or gate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Any market: "no sponsorship", "must have right to work", "EU residents only", security clearance, required language the candidate lacks | Gate (G=0) | Legal impossibility or knockout |
| UK: sponsorship stated, salary ≥ £41,700 | 0.8 | Explicit offer; employer has priced in the ISC |
| UK: employer on sponsor register, posting silent, salary ≥ £41,700 | 0.25 | Can sponsor, but £1,320/yr cost deters junior and mid roles |
| UK: not on register, or salary < £41,700 (non-ISL) | Gate | Unsponsorable for new entrants |
| Germany: English posting, ICT role, salary ≥ €45,934 or not stated | 0.7 | Low-cost Blue Card route |
| Germany: stated salary < €45,934 | 0.3 | Below Blue Card shortage threshold |
| Switzerland: large international employer or relocation/permit mentioned | 0.35 | Labour-market test is feasible for large firms |
| Switzerland: small company, posting silent | 0.05 | Priority-test burden rarely worth it |
| Spain: English posting, salary ≥ Blue Card threshold | 0.6 | Lower thresholds; language often required |
| Remote "EMEA" | 0.6 | Turkey is often inside EMEA EOR coverage |
| Remote "worldwide", contractor/B2B, or via Deel/Remote/Oyster | 1.0 (competition still applies) | No location friction; 8.8× applicants handled by m_comp |
| "CET ±2–3 h" time-zone constraint | 0.9 | Turkey is UTC+3; a time-zone rule, not a residency rule |
| Turkey domestic | 1.0 | No friction |

## Value should be log pay plus growth, stability and flexibility, with floors

The job-quality frameworks argue against reducing value to pay. The OECD measures **three dimensions: earnings quality, labour-market security and working-environment quality** ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/measuring-and-assessing-job-quality_5jrp02kjw1mr-en.html)). Eurofound tracks seven, and deliberately refuses to combine them into one composite index **"as clearly there are trade-offs"** between them ([Eurofound](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/blog/2023/job-quality-pivotal-addressing-todays-workplace-and-societal-challenges)). For a scorer, that means a weighted sum with floor caps: a very bad dimension, such as pay below the candidate's minimum or a disqualifying work arrangement, should cap value rather than be averaged away.

Evidence on what predicts satisfaction and retention points the same way:

- **Glassdoor (2023 driver decomposition):** culture and values carry about 18.4% of satisfaction, senior leadership 17.6%, career opportunities 15.8% and compensation about 11% ([Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/satisfaction-drivers-remain-intact/); verified only through a secondary summary).
- **MIT Sloan Management Review:** **toxic culture was 10.4 times more powerful than compensation** in predicting company-level attrition ([MIT SMR](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/toxic-culture-is-driving-the-great-resignation/)).
- **Work Institute:** career development is the most cited reason for quitting every year since 2010, cited by 18.9% of leavers in 2024 ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/career-development-gaps-frequently-drive-employee-turnover)).

Culture is mostly invisible in a posting, though, and pay is visible and decisive when the candidate accepts an offer. The recommended weights therefore lean on pay while keeping growth and stability substantial.

Pay should enter as a logarithm relative to a reference. Experience-sampling data shows **well-being rises roughly linearly in log income, with no plateau for most people** ([Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10068796/)). The recommended form is:

```
x       = ln(offer_expected / ref)
v_pay   = clamp01(0.5 + x / (2·ln 1.5))   if x ≥ 0
v_pay   = clamp01(0.5 + x / ln 1.5)       if x < 0
ref     = max(candidate's current net pay, market median for the role and location)
```

A raise of 50% scores 1.0. Offers below the reference fall twice as steeply, a loss-aversion assumption that has not been tested directly. A market median per location absorbs most cost-of-living differences between Istanbul and Zurich. offer_expected should be the midpoint or lower third of the posted range. Colorado's transparency law raised **posted salaries 3.6% but realized salaries only about 1.3%**, and raised the share of postings with ranges by about 30 percentage points ([Arnold, Quach & Taska, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34480)). The EU Pay Transparency Directive's transposition deadline was **7 June 2026** ([Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/pay-transparency-compliance-underway-across-the-eu/)). Missing pay is therefore a mild negative and a source of uncertainty in EU states that require ranges, and neutral elsewhere. For Turkish contractor roles, compute net pay after the 100% service-export deduction.

Growth, stability and flexibility each have one strong anchor:

- **Growth.** Starting a career at a larger firm raises lifetime earnings even after the worker moves on, through better skill development ([Arellano-Bover, JOLE 2024](https://docs.iza.org/dp12969.pdf)). Early-career candidates should therefore weight employer quality and learning more heavily. The "stretch sweet spot", 60–80% skill overlap plus 1–3 new core skills, is an assumption to tune on outcomes, not a research finding.
- **Stability.** Carta counted **966 startup shutdowns in 2024, up 25.6%**, and only **15.4% of the Q1 2022 seed cohort reached Series A within two years, against 30.6% for Q1 2018** ([TechCrunch on Carta](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/26/2025-will-likely-be-another-brutal-year-of-failed-startups-data-suggests); [Carta](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-seed-2022-only-24-percent-reach-series-a/)). Funding stage is therefore the best observable stability proxy.
- **Flexibility.** A randomized controlled trial with 1,612 employees found that **two work-from-home days a week cut quits by one-third** with no harm to promotions ([Bloom et al., Nature 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11208135/)). A co-author is the firm's co-founder.
- **Reviews.** Glassdoor rating *changes*, driven by the career-opportunity and leadership sub-ratings, predicted stock returns ([Green et al. 2019](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/qw50/EmpReviews.pdf)). Later work questions that result's robustness. Glassdoor's terms also prohibit automated scraping ([Glassdoor Terms](https://glassdoor.com/about/terms.htm)), so review data must stay optional and user-supplied, with its weight redistributed when it is missing.

| Value component | Default weight | Scoring rule |
|---|---|---|
| Pay v_pay | 0.35 | Log ratio to reference with loss-aversion kink; net of tax regime |
| Growth and learning | 0.22 | Employer size and quality, learning signals, stretch band; +0.08 shifted from pay for early-career users |
| Stability | 0.18 | Pre-seed/seed 0.3, Series A 0.45, B–C 0.65, late-stage/public 0.85; −0.15 after a layoff in the last 12 months |
| Work arrangement | 0.13 | Matches stated remote/hybrid preference 1.0; one step off 0.6; opposite 0.2 (a refused arrangement is a gate) |
| Culture and reputation | 0.12 | User-supplied ratings shrunk toward a prior with 20–50 pseudo-reviews; trend weighted over level |
| Floor rule | — | If pay is below the user's minimum or the contract is insecure, V ≤ 0.3 |

A user-weighted "route value" term is a product decision outside this evidence base. It would cover, for example, a Blue Card job as a path to EU residence, and it should be a visible preference, not a hidden weight.

## Combine with an expected-surplus index, then calibrate

The decision literature supports two stages. As the number of options grows, people switch to strategies that **"eliminate some of the available alternatives as quickly as possible"** ([Payne 1976](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/792478)). Tversky formalized this as elimination by aspects ([Tversky 1972](https://www.behavioraleconomics.com/resources/mini-encyclopedia-of-be/elimination-by-aspects/)). The gates do that elimination. Among the jobs that survive, the method has to stay stable as the feed changes daily. TOPSIS ranks jobs by distance to the ideal and anti-ideal points of the current set, so it can reorder jobs whose own data never changed when others are added or removed. Simple additive weighting (SAW) does not suffer this rank reversal ([Annals of OR 2023](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/199927/1/s10479-023-05339-w.pdf)). Value should therefore use SAW inside its block. The top level should be multiplicative, consistent with Keeney's multiplicative multiattribute utility ([Keeney 1974](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/oropre/v22y1974i1p22-34.html)), so that a near-zero chance cannot be rescued by high value.

The top-level index should be the one Bied et al. derived from a search model. In that model, the reservation utility rises as hiring probability falls, U₀*(p) = rV₀ − R̄ + (k̄ + R̄)/p, where k̄ and R̄ are the scaled costs of applying and of being rejected. The welfare-optimal ranking is **Γ(p,U) = p · σ · log(1 + exp((U − U₀*(p))/σ))**, a softplus of the surplus multiplied by the hiring probability. The authors show that ranking by utility alone, by hiring probability alone, or by observed applications is generically suboptimal ([Bied et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21699)). The implementation-ready version is:

```
Γ = P_eff · σ · ln(1 + exp((V − v0 − c / P_eff) / σ))
defaults: σ = 0.05, v0 = user reservation value (e.g. 0.45), c = 0.001 (Easy Apply) to 0.003 (tailored application)
```

The cost term c is where the evidence becomes a product insight. **The "ambition versus safety" slider is really the cost per application.** When applying is cheap, long shots stay worthwhile. When each application needs tailoring, the bar for low-chance jobs rises steeply, as 1/p.

The worked example below uses the factor tables above. Job A is a Berlin Series C role in English at €65k, three days old, with 85% must-have coverage: P = 0.08 × 0.9 × 0.7 × 0.9 ≈ 0.045. Job B is a worldwide-remote role at a famous AI lab, ten days old. Job C is a London role at a licensed sponsor whose posting is silent on sponsorship, 40 days old. Job D is an Istanbul onsite role. Ranked by value alone, the order is B, C, A, D. Ranked by chance alone, it is D, A, B, C. The index puts the realistic, well-paid German role first and the dream jobs last, exactly the divergence Bied et al. observed.

| Job | P_eff | V | U₀ = v0 + c/P | Γ × 1000 | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Berlin, English, €65k, day 3 | 0.045 | 0.66 | 0.472 | 8.5 | 1 |
| D: Istanbul onsite | 0.065 | 0.50 | 0.465 | 3.6 | 2 |
| B: worldwide remote, famous lab, day 10 | 0.005 | 0.92 | 0.650 | 1.4 | 3 |
| C: London, silent sponsor, day 40 | 0.004 | 0.80 | 0.700 | 0.4 | 4 |

A ranked list is not the same as the right set of applications. Chade and Smith show that when several applications go out at once and only the best success counts, the optimal portfolio comes from a **greedy marginal-improvement algorithm, and it can skip middle-ranked options** ([Chade & Smith 2006](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2006/09/01/simultaneous-search)). The weekly shortlist should therefore be built greedily. Add next the job that most raises the expected best accepted value, net of its cost, given the jobs already chosen. Treat jobs that share a failure mode (same employer, same visa constraint) as correlated. The result is a deliberate mix of a few reach jobs, mostly target jobs and some safety jobs.

Production systems show where this should go. LinkedIn's GLMix adds per-member and per-job random effects to a global model and produced **20–40% more job applications** ([AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/57aa28de0a3ac518da989745/glmix-generalized-linear-mixed-models-for-large-scale-response-prediction)). LinkedIn's recruiter-side online-learning features lifted NDCG@1 by **49.61%** ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/learning/learning-hiring-preferences-the-ai-behind-linkedin-jobs)). In the cold-start RecSys 2017 challenge, content-based profile-to-job similarity won ([Volkovs et al. 2017](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~guangweiyu/pdfs/recsys2017.pdf)). The weighting prior comes from Dawes: **unit weights on standardized, correctly signed predictors are robust** and often match optimized weights out of sample ([Dawes 1979](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf)).

Calibration follows directly from these results. The log of the multiplicative chance model is a linear predictor in which every log-multiplier has a coefficient of 1. Learning therefore means fitting those exponents by logistic regression on outcomes, with ridge shrinkage toward 1. That is Dawes-style robustness, and GLMix-style per-user deviations come later. Platt scaling maps a score to a probability with two parameters ([Platt scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platt_scaling)), and Bied et al. used exactly this logistic step. Isotonic regression is worth using only with plenty of data ([Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)).

A practical caveat follows from the base rate. At 5–8% interview rates, 50 applications yield only 2–4 interviews, too few to calibrate one user's interview probability. The early calibration target should be "any human response". In the Belgian data, positive reactions run about two to three times as often as interview invitations (22–27% against 8–12%) ([Baert & Verhaest 2014](https://repec.iza.org/dp8312.pdf)). Interview calibration should be pooled across users until each user has hundreds of outcomes. P(offer) should be chained as P(interview | apply) × P(offer | interview), with each stage calibrated separately.

Until calibration exists, the interface should show a percentile within the active pool next to an absolute chance band, not a fake probability. Robustness should be checked by perturbing weights by ±20–50%, or sampling them from a Dirichlet distribution around the defaults, and reporting how often each job stays in the top k.

## Conclusion

For this candidate profile, the scorer's real job is triage by legal feasibility and congestion, not skill matching. The market and crowding factors (0.25 for a UK posting that is silent on sponsorship, 0.27 for worldwide remote, 0.05 for a small Swiss firm) are as large as the gap between a strong and a weak skill match, and they hit exactly the jobs where the candidate's skills fit best. A good match on a UK posting with no sponsor signal is worth less than a moderate match on an English-language German posting above the Blue Card threshold. Two design consequences follow. The system has to read sponsorship, residency and language clauses with near-perfect recall, because each one is a gate rather than a feature. And it must measure posting age from when it first saw a job, because both the timing advantage and the ghost risk depend on that date.

The deeper shift is from "how well do I match?" to "is this application worth its cost?" The expected-surplus index makes that trade-off explicit through one parameter that users can understand, the cost per application. The greedy portfolio replaces a sorted list with a deliberate mix of reach, target and safety jobs. What remains uncertain is quantitative: the shape of the must-have curve, the size of the experience-gap penalty, the competition elasticity and every market multiplier are priors without public field estimates. Turkey's domestic market is barely covered by the evidence. The highest-value next step is therefore not more research but instrumentation. Log every application's factor values and its outcome stage, so that within a few hundred pooled applications the priors become fitted coefficients shrunk toward these defaults.
