# Fit and Screening: What Predicts an Applicant Getting an Interview or Offer (Candidate Side)

Notes compiled 2026-11-23. Sources marked "[not re-fetched]" are standard, well-known literature cited from prior knowledge. Their numbers were not re-verified in this session, so check them before quoting.

## Q1. Person–job vs person–organization fit: which fit types predict hiring, performance, satisfaction and turnover?

### Takeaway
Fit predicts recruiters' hiring intent strongly. Person–job (PJ) fit correlates ρ≈.67 and person–organization (PO) fit ρ≈.61 with a recruiter's intent to hire. Both predict job performance only weakly (PJ ρ≈.20, PO ρ≈.07) and actual turnover very weakly (PJ −.08, PO −.14). Fit that is computed objectively (like an algorithm's match score) predicts outcomes much less well than fit that people perceive directly. For pre-entry outcomes the gap is very large: PO fit → job offer is .50 when perceived and .03 when measured indirectly.

### Cited Findings
- **Study scope:** Kristof-Brown, Zimmerman & Johnson (2005), *Personnel Psychology* 58:281–342. 172 studies, 836 effect sizes, mostly US samples. It covers PJ, PO, person–group (PG) and person–supervisor (PS) fit, with pre-entry criteria (applicant attraction, job acceptance, intent to hire, job offer) and post-entry criteria. Nearly all 80% credibility intervals exclude 0. — [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005 (PDF)](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **Pre-entry outcomes (closest to "chance of interview or offer"):**
  - PJ fit: ρ=.48 with organizational attraction, ρ=.67 with the organization's intent to hire.
  - PO fit: .46 with attraction, .24 with applicant job acceptance, .61 with intent to hire, .32 with actual job offers.
  - Source: [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **Measurement method changes pre-entry effects a lot (PO fit, direct perceived vs indirect):** attraction .62 vs .22, intent to hire .70 vs .18, job offer .50 vs .03. The authors conclude that recruiters' and applicants' PO-fit perceptions "have little, if any, connection with reality." Recruiter PO-fit judgments may reflect "similar-to-me" bias. — [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **PJ fit, post-entry outcomes:**
  - Job satisfaction .56 (k=47, N=12,960)
  - Organizational commitment .47
  - Intent to quit −.46
  - Overall performance .20; the 80% credibility interval is −.04 to .44, so the effect does not generalize reliably
  - Tenure .18
  - Actual turnover −.08
  - Strain −.28
  - Source: [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **PO fit, post-entry outcomes:**
  - Job satisfaction .44
  - Organizational commitment .51
  - Organizational satisfaction .65
  - Overall performance .07
  - Task performance .13
  - Contextual performance .27
  - Tenure .03
  - Turnover −.14
  - Source: [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **PG and PS fit:**
  - PG fit: satisfaction .31, intent to quit −.22, overall performance .19, tenure .06.
  - PS fit: job satisfaction .44, overall performance .18 (.31 when perceived directly vs .11 when measured indirectly).
  - Source: [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **Demands–abilities (D–A) vs needs–supplies (N–S) PJ fit:**

  | Outcome | Combined | N–S | D–A |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Job satisfaction | .62 | .61 | .41 |
  | Organizational commitment | .51 | .37 | .31 |
  | Intent to quit | −.57 | −.50 | −.23 |
  | Overall performance | — | .20 | .12 |

  The authors say the D–A fit "has a minor impact on attitudes and even performance when compared with the needs–supplies fit." — [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **Objective vs perceived PJ fit:**
  - Job satisfaction: objective (computed P vs E comparison) .28, direct perceived .58, indirect subjective .59 (Table 1).
  - Intent to quit: perceived −.49, subjective −.44, objective −.18.
  - Overall performance: .22 / .20 / .16 in the same order.
  - Source: [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **How the fit types relate to each other:** PJ–PO .72. Demands–abilities PJ relates to PO at only .45, while needs–supplies PJ relates to PO at .73. — [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **Asymmetry in polynomial-regression studies:** "Excesses of E are generally not as detrimental as excesses of P." Fit at high levels (high P and high E) is generally better than fit at low levels. — [Kristof-Brown et al. 2005](https://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Meta_person_job_fit.pdf)
- **Selection-validity benchmarks for scale [not re-fetched]:**
  - Schmidt & Hunter (1998), *Psychological Bulletin* 124(2): general mental ability .51, structured interview .51, work sample .54, years of job experience .18, years of education .10.
  - Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022), *J. Applied Psychology*, revised most of these validities downward (for example, structured interview ≈.42, GMA ≈.31).
  - Van Iddekinge et al. (2019), *Personnel Psychology*: pre-hire work experience predicts performance only weakly (ρ≈.06).
  - Sources: [Schmidt & Hunter 1998 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.124.2.262); [Sackett et al. 2022 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994); [Van Iddekinge et al. 2019 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12335)

### Inferences
- **The "chance of interview/offer" component should model what screeners perceive, not true fit.**
  - Perceived PJ fit with the hiring side is strongly tied to intent to hire (.67–.70).
  - Objective PO fit is nearly unrelated to offers (.03).
  - So surface signals matter most for callbacks: title match, visible keyword/skill overlap, and seniority match. Deep culture or values matching matters much less.
- **A "fit" score that tries to predict the candidate's later satisfaction should weight needs–supplies factors heavily** (salary, remote work, growth, domain interest). N–S is roughly 1.5× D–A for satisfaction and about 2× for intent to quit. Demands–abilities (skills) should dominate only the interview-chance side.
- **Correlations of .07–.20 with performance and −.08 to −.14 with turnover mean fit is a weak lever for long-term outcomes.** Present the score as "likely to be screened in," not "you will succeed."
- **Being somewhat overqualified (P > E) is penalized less than being underqualified.** This supports an asymmetric penalty around the requirement: gentle above it, steep below it. Q4 adds a commitment-based caveat at large overshoots.

### Gaps
- The meta-analysis is from 2005 and mostly US. I found no comparable recent meta-analysis focused specifically on tech or knowledge-work roles.
- The PJ "job offer" coefficient could not be extracted cleanly from the garbled table text. The PO fit → job offer figure (.32) is reported in the paper's prose.

## Q2. How recruiters and ATS screen: must-haves, knockouts, time per resume, keyword matching

### Takeaway
The first human pass is very short (about 6–7.4 seconds) and concentrates on job titles. ATS screens rely heavily on negative or knockout filters, and almost all employers admit these reject qualified people who don't match exactly. Exact title match is the strongest vendor-observed correlate of getting an interview, but that evidence is observational.

### Cited Findings
- **Ladders eye-tracking studies:**
  - 2012: about 6 seconds per resume, with 30 recruiters.
  - 2018 update: average initial screen of 7.4 seconds. Ladders partly attributes the 2012 figure to recession-era application volume.
  - Recruiters looked at job titles more than any other element. Simple layouts with clear headings held attention.
  - This is company-published, small-sample (US) research.
  - Sources: [HR Dive on Ladders 2018](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/); [Ladders 2018 PDF](https://www.bu.edu/com/files/2018/10/TheLadders-EyeTracking-StudyC2.pdf)
- **Harvard Business School & Accenture, "Hidden Workers: Untapped Talent" (Sept 2021):**
  - Survey of 8,720 workers and 2,275 executives in the US, UK and Germany.
  - 88% of employers said qualified high-skill candidates are rejected because they don't exactly match the criteria; 94% said the same for middle-skill candidates.
  - ATS rely on "negative" filters such as "exclude candidates without college degrees."
  - These are employer perceptions, not a measured rate of false rejection.
  - Sources: [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/how-to-tap-the-talent-automated-hr-platforms-miss); [Report PDF via SHRM](https://nahr.shrm.org/sites/default/files/hiddenworkers09032021.pdf); [HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/report-applicant-tracking-systems-may-exclude-whole-segments-of-candidates/606614/)
- **ATS adoption:** 99% of Fortune 500 firms used an ATS in 2019, according to Jobscan. Jobscan's 2025 check identified the ATS at 492 of the Fortune 500. — [WEF citing Jobscan](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/artificial-intelligence-job-recruitment-process/); [Jobscan 2025](https://jobscan.co/blog/interview-rates-study)
- **Jobscan vendor analysis (Feb 2025; more than 2.5M applications tracked through its tools; no causal design):**
  - Exact job-title match: 10.6× higher interview rate. An older Jobscan claim says 3.5×, and the discrepancy is unexplained.
  - Cover letter: 3.4×.
  - Optimized LinkedIn profile: 2.2×.
  - Bachelor's degree: 6.1×. Master's or doctorate: 6.7×.
  - These are heavily confounded by self-selection. Treat them as direction only, not magnitude.
  - Source: [Jobscan interview rates study 2025](https://jobscan.co/blog/interview-rates-study)

### Inferences
- **Treat explicit hard requirements as near-binary gates in the interview-chance component, not as linear contributors.** These include work authorization, location or onsite requirements, required language, and a required degree where the posting enforces one. When a gate fails, multiply the interview-chance estimate toward roughly zero rather than subtracting a few points.
- **Title and seniority similarity deserves a large weight in callback chance.** Recruiters spend their few seconds on titles, and title match is the strongest (if confounded) vendor correlate.

### Gaps
- **Knockout questions:** I found no public quantitative data on how often knockout questions are used or what they do to applicant outcomes (work authorization, years of experience, location). The vendors (Workday, Greenhouse, iCIMS) don't publish this.
- **Keyword-match thresholds:** I found no peer-reviewed data on the keyword or skill-match thresholds ATS ranking uses. Popular "aim for a 75–80% match score" advice is vendor folklore with no verifiable provenance.

## Q3. Partial skill match: the 60%/100% claim, LinkedIn data, skills-based hiring

### Takeaway
The "men apply at 60%, women only at 100% of requirements" statistic has no verifiable provenance. It comes from an unpublished internal HP report. LinkedIn's behavioral data does show women apply to about 20% fewer jobs yet are about 16% more likely to be hired when they apply. That fits the idea that self-screening is too strict. I found no public data on hire or interview rates by share of requirements met.

### Cited Findings
- **The 60%/100% claim:**
  - Origin: an unpublished internal Hewlett-Packard report, popularized by Sandberg's *Lean In* and Mohr's HBR article (Aug 2014).
  - The Behavioural Insights Team (UK, March 2022) calls it frequently cited but unsubstantiated.
  - BIT found that nudging women to weigh individual requirements may reduce the application gap.
  - Sources: [BIT 2022 report PDF](https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Gender-differences-in-response-to-requirements-in-job-adverts-March-2022.pdf); [Her Campus summary](https://www.hercampus.com/?p=1824503); [Australian DEWR copy of Mohr HBR](https://www.dewr.gov.au/download/11828/why-women-dont-apply-jobs-unless-theyre-100-qualified/22483/why-women-dont-apply-jobs-unless-theyre-100-qualified/pdf)
- **Mohr's own survey [not re-fetched]:** about 1,000 mostly US professionals. "I didn't think they would hire me since I didn't meet the qualifications" was the top reason for not applying for both genders, at roughly 41% of women and 46% of men. This is self-report about beliefs, not hiring outcomes. — [Mohr HBR 2014](https://hbr.org/2014/08/why-women-dont-apply-for-jobs-unless-theyre-100-qualified)
- **LinkedIn Gender Insights Report (2019, global LinkedIn behavioral data):**
  - Women apply to about 20% fewer jobs than men.
  - Women are 16% less likely to apply after viewing a job. Some coverage says 14%, so the figure is inconsistent across sources.
  - Women are 16% more likely to be hired after applying, and 18% more likely for roles more senior than their current one, per the report.
  - Sources: [LinkedIn blog 2019](https://business.linkedin.com/talent-solutions/blog/diversity/2019/how-women-find-jobs-gender-report); [Report PDF](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/me/business/en-us/talent-solutions-lodestone/body/pdf/Gender-Insights-Report.pdf)
- **LinkedIn Economic Graph "Skills-First" (2023):**
  - Skills-based hiring could expand US talent pools by about 19× on average, and by up to about 20× in other coverage.
  - Digital Marketing Manager: about 22×.
  - Workers without a bachelor's degree: about 9×.
  - The method counts people who held roles in the last 5 years with relevant skills.
  - The 2025 update (Mar 2025) reports smaller average expansions of about 4.3–4.4× when comparing against degree- or title-based pools.
  - These are simulated pool sizes, not hire rates.
  - Sources: [Skills-First 2023 PDF](https://economicgraphchallenge.linkedin.com:443/content/dam/me/economicgraph/en-us/PDF/skills-first-report-2023.pdf); [TechRepublic coverage](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/skills-first-hiring-linkedin/); [Skills-based hiring Mar 2025 PDF](https://economicgraph.linkedin.com/content/dam/me/economicgraph/en-us/PDF/skills-based-hiring-march-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The interview-chance function should not require a 100% requirement match.** It should not drop off a cliff at 60% either, because that number has no empirical basis.
  - A reasonable form is a smooth, saturating curve (for example a logistic) on the weighted match of *must-have* skills.
  - Nice-to-haves should get a much lower weight.
  - Hard gates (Q2) sit outside this curve.
- **Self-screening appears too strict on average** (women's higher hire rate per application). The product can nudge candidates to apply to jobs with strong but incomplete matches.

### Gaps
- **Hire or interview rates by share of requirements met:** I found no public dataset. LinkedIn, Indeed and Greenhouse have this internally but haven't published it.
- The HP report itself is unavailable.

## Q4. Over- and under-qualification, experience gaps, seniority mismatch

### Takeaway
The evidence on mild overqualification is mixed to positive.
- In a Belgian field experiment, master's graduates applying to bachelor-level jobs got about 19% more direct interview invitations, especially in shortage occupations.
- Lab experiments with US hiring managers show a commitment-based penalty for overqualified men, but not for women.
- A history of past overeducation is penalized less than past unemployment.

### Cited Findings
- **Verhaest, Bogaert, Dereymaeker, Mestdagh & Baert (2018), *Industrial Relations* 57(3):**
  - Design: paired fictitious applications from bachelor's and master's graduates to real Belgian (Flemish) vacancies requiring a bachelor's.
  - Overqualified master's graduates were 19% more likely to be directly invited to interview and 11% more likely to get any positive reaction.
  - The advantage was larger in bottleneck (shortage) occupations and varied across employers.
  - The advantage appeared mainly in jobs with high overall invitation rates.
  - Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:indres:v:57:y:2018:i:3:p:361-388); [IZA DP 9654](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/9654/crowding-out-in-the-labour-market-do-employers-lend-a-hand)
- **Baert & Verhaest (IZA DP 8312, July 2014), Flanders, Belgium:**
  - Trios of fictitious male applications to 540 vacancies.
  - Any positive reaction: school-leaver 27%, formerly unemployed 22%, formerly overeducated 25%.
  - Interview invitations: 12%, 8% and 10% respectively.
  - Unemployment history led to 12% fewer positive callbacks than overeducation history (p<.05).
  - The overeducation stigma was significant for permanent-contract jobs only.
  - Source: [IZA DP 8312](https://repec.iza.org/dp8312.pdf)
- **Hahl, Guo, Galperin & Sterling, "Too Good to Hire?" (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2019):**
  - Four experiments with hiring managers.
  - Highly capable or overqualified candidates were seen as less committed and penalized, partly because managers assume they have better outside options (flight risk).
  - The effect reverses for women in child-rearing years.
  - A follow-up in *Organization Science* (215 US hiring managers) found that overqualification raised perceived career commitment for women only.
  - Sources: [CMU news 2019](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2019/july/overqualified-job-seekers.html); [Organization Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/orsc.2021.1550); [HKS Gender Action Portal](https://gap.hks.harvard.edu/hes-overqualified-shes-highly-committed-qualification-signals-and-gendered-assumptions-about-job)
- **Kulkarni, Lengnick-Hall & Martinez (2015), *Personnel Review*:** interviews with 24 US hiring managers. Managers will interview or hire overqualified applicants only if they have "sufficient compensatory qualities." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108%2FPR-11-2013-0204)

### Inferences
- **Under-qualification penalty should be steep; mild over-qualification penalty should be small or zero.** One overshoot step (for example one degree level, or a few years over the minimum) is neutral to positive for callbacks, especially in shortage fields like many tech roles. Large overshoots (applying well below one's level) need a moderate penalty for perceived flight risk.
- **Applying above one's level is a separate dimension that the reviewed studies did not quantify.** LinkedIn's report suggests women who stretch-apply are hired at higher rates, but that comes from a selected sample.

### Gaps
- I found no correspondence study that isolates callback rates by years-of-experience shortfall relative to a posting's stated "X+ years." The shape of the experience-gap penalty is therefore unsupported by direct field evidence.
- I found no US or tech-specific field experiment on overqualification callbacks. The Belgian evidence may not transfer.

## Q5. Other strong callback predictors: referrals, tailoring, title, prestige, baseline callback rates

### Takeaway
Referrals are the largest candidate-controllable multiplier.
- Referral hire rates are roughly 1 in 10 (ERIN 2024), versus about 1 in 150 for cold career-site applications (Lever 2016).
- Baseline callback rates for cold online applications are low: roughly 6–25% depending on country, era and how "callback" is defined. Hire rates are about 1%.

### Cited Findings
- **Burks, Cowgill, Hoffman & Housman (2015), *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(2):**
  - Data from 9 large firms in call centers, trucking and high-tech, US.
  - Referred applicants are more likely to be hired and to accept offers despite similar skill characteristics.
  - Referred workers are substantially less likely to quit. They produce more patents in high-tech.
  - Referrals from longer-tenured employees are more valuable.
  - A later paper cites referred workers as 21–39% more profitable.
  - Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:2:p:805-839); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-value-of-hiring-through-employee-referrals/)
- **Lever (2016 data, about 4M candidates, US-centric):**
  - Agency candidates: 1 in 22 hired.
  - Sourced candidates: 1 in 72.
  - Career-site or posting applicants: 1 in 152.
  - The referral rate is commonly reported as about 1 in 16, but I didn't confirm it in the snippet.
  - Source: [SHRM on Lever](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/lever-study-shows-1-100-candidates-hired)
- **ERIN (2024 data, about 1.1M referrals, enterprise orgs):**
  - About 1 in 10 referrals is hired, versus 50–60 job-board applicants per hire.
  - Funnel: of 10 referred, 6 apply, 4 are interviewed, 1 is hired. So referred applicants are interviewed at roughly 67%.
  - Vendor data.
  - Source: [SHRM](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/employee-referrals-remain-top-source-hires)
- **Glassdoor (2015, self-reported interview reviews):** referrals are associated with a 2.6–6.6 percentage point higher chance of an accepted offer, all else equal. Online applications are associated with lower offer probability. — [Glassdoor Research](https://www.glassdoor.com/research/interview-sources/)
- **Jobvite (undated, second-hand):** referrals are 40% of successful hires, the internal careers site 21%, job boards 15%. — [NZ Herald citing Jobvite](https://nzherald.co.nz/business/tom-oneil-hiring-staff-ask-your-team-first/4VHMARM6Z24M2OBX2GCUVCEXVE)
- **Kline, Rose & Walters (2022), *QJE* (NBER w29053):**
  - Over 83,000 fictitious applications to 108 of the largest US employers (2019–2021).
  - Distinctively Black names lowered employer contact by 2.1 percentage points.
  - The between-firm standard deviation of the gap is 1.9 points. The top quintile of firms accounts for about half of lost contacts.
  - Gender gap: not significant on average, but the between-firm standard deviation is 2.7 points.
  - The abstract doesn't state the baseline contact rate. I recall it as roughly 25% for white names [not verified].
  - Sources: [NBER w29053](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29053); [BFI summary](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/a-discrimination-report-card/)
- **Bertrand & Mullainathan (2004), *American Economic Review* [not re-fetched]:**
  - About 5,000 resumes to about 1,300 ads in Boston and Chicago, 2001–02.
  - Callback rate 9.65% for white names vs 6.45% for Black names, about 50% more callbacks.
  - Sources: [NBER w9873](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9873); [Chicago Booth summary](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/a-discrimination-report-card/)
- **Belgian benchmark:** callback 22–27% for any positive reaction, 8–12% for interview invitations (Baert & Verhaest 2014). — [IZA DP 8312](https://repec.iza.org/dp8312.pdf)
- **Tailoring and cover letters:** evidence is only vendor-observational. Jobscan reports 3.4× for a cover letter and 10.6× for a title match (Q2). — [Jobscan 2025](https://jobscan.co/blog/interview-rates-study)

### Inferences
- **"Referral available" (a known contact at the company) is the single most powerful callback multiplier.**
  - Hire odds are about 10–15× those of a cold application (1/10–1/16 vs 1/152).
  - Interview conversion is likely more than 5× higher.
  - Model it as multiplicative on the baseline, not additive.
- **For a calibrated "chance of interview," a cold-application baseline of about 5–12% is defensible.** It should then be scaled by the gates (Q2), the must-have match curve (Q3), title and seniority alignment, and the referral multiplier. Large firms show significant firm-level variation.
- **Vendor multipliers (title 10.6×, cover letter 3.4×) are too confounded to use directly.** Use them only as an ordering: title match is a stronger signal than a cover letter.

### Gaps
- **Prestige:** I found no verified effect sizes for prior-employer prestige or for education prestige in tech hiring in this session. Kessler, Low & Sullivan (2019, AER, incentivized resume rating) is the relevant source but wasn't read. — [NBER w25800](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25800/w25800.pdf)
- **Tailoring:** I found no randomized field experiment on resume tailoring or cover letters' causal effect on callbacks.
- **Europe and Turkey:** I found no referral or callback data specific to these markets beyond the Belgian studies.
